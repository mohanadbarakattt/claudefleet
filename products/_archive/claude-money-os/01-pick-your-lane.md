# 01 — Pick Your Lane

You cannot build a business around "AI stuff in general." You build it around one lane, one offer, one buyer. This chapter gives you five proven lanes, a matrix to score them against your actual situation, and an exercise that ends with you committing to one.

## The five lanes

### Lane 1: AI-assisted freelance services
You sell a service — copywriting, research, data cleanup, content repurposing, admin support, translation, basic analysis — where Claude does most of the execution and you do the judgment, quality control, and client relationship. This is the fastest lane to a first dollar because the buyer already exists: people are hiring for these tasks on Upwork, Fiverr, and via referral every day. Your edge is speed and consistency, not "I can write," but "I can turn your rough notes into a polished 2,000-word case study in 24 hours, every time."

### Lane 2: Digital products
You build a one-time-effort, sell-forever asset: a template pack, a swipe file, a planner, a mini-course, a prompt library, a niche guide (like this one). Claude accelerates the creation — drafting, structuring, editing — but the business model is different from services: you make it once, you sell it many times, and your income is decoupled from your hours. Slower to first dollar (you need an audience or a launch channel), but the best long-run leverage of the five lanes.

### Lane 3: Content-to-audience-to-offer
You use Claude to produce content (newsletter, short-form video scripts, LinkedIn posts, a niche blog) at a pace and quality you couldn't sustain solo, build an audience around a specific topic, and eventually sell that audience a service, product, or affiliate offer. This is the slowest lane to money but the most durable — an audience is an asset that compounds. Only pick this if you can commit to months of consistent output before expecting revenue.

### Lane 4: Local business services
You sell AI-powered deliverables to local businesses who have money but no time or skill to do this themselves: a restaurant's Google Business Q&A responses, a dentist's review-response workflow, a contractor's quote-follow-up emails, a salon's social captions. Claude does the drafting; you do the sales (usually in person or by phone) and the account management. Slower to first dollar than Lane 1 because you're doing cold local outreach instead of browsing an existing marketplace, but the deals tend to be recurring monthly retainers, not one-off gigs.

### Lane 5: Micro-SaaS with Claude Code
You build a small, focused software tool — using Claude Code as your engineering team — and sell subscriptions. Highest ceiling, highest skill requirement, longest and least certain path to first dollar. Only pick this if you're willing to spend weeks before any revenue, and only if you already have a specific, validated problem in mind (not "an app idea"). This lane eats people who start here without having done Lane 1 or 2 first — the muscle of packaging an offer and finding a buyer is the same, and it's cheaper to learn it in a faster-cycle lane.

## Decision matrix

Score each lane 1 (low) to 5 (high) against your actual constraints — not what sounds impressive.

| Lane | Time to learn | Skill required | Capital needed | Speed to first dollar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Freelance services | Low (1) | Low–Med (2) | None (1) | Fast (1–2 weeks) |
| 2. Digital products | Med (3) | Med (3) | Low (2) | Med (3–6 weeks) |
| 3. Content → audience | Med (3) | Med (3) | Low (2) | Slow (2–4 months) |
| 4. Local business services | Med (2) | Low–Med (2) | Low (2) | Med (3–5 weeks) |
| 5. Micro-SaaS | High (5) | High (5) | Low–Med (3) | Slow (2–4 months) |

Lower numbers in the first three columns and "faster" in the last column are easier for a beginner. If you have less than 10 hours a week and need income in 30 days, Lane 1 wins almost every time. If you have an existing audience or a strong pre-existing niche interest, Lane 2 or 3 might beat it. If you're comfortable with cold outreach and like the idea of recurring local contracts, Lane 4 is underrated. Lane 5 is a second-business move, not a first one, for most readers.

## The pick-one exercise

Do this now, on paper or in a note. Do not proceed to chapter 2 until you've written an answer to each:

1. **Hours available per week for this, honestly**: ______
2. **Money I can put in before I need to see a return**: ______
3. **Skill I already have that's closest to sellable** (writing, research, design sense, sales, a specific industry background): ______
4. **How fast do I need a first dollar** (this week / this month / I can wait 3+ months): ______
5. Cross-reference 1–4 against the matrix above. Circle the lane with the fewest mismatches.
6. Write one sentence: "I am building a [lane] business around [specific skill or topic from #3]."

That sentence is your starting constraint for chapter 2. If you can't finish it, the honest move is to pick Lane 1 by default — it has the fewest ways to go wrong and the fastest feedback loop, and everything you learn there (packaging, delivery, client handling) transfers directly to any lane you move into later.

Do not pick two lanes and split your effort. A diluted business is a slower business. Pick one, run it for at least 30 days, and only reconsider once you have real signal — replies, calls, a client — not before.
