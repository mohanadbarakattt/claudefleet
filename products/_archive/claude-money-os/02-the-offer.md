# 02 — The Offer

A lane is a category. An offer is the specific thing a specific person pays you a specific amount to get by a specific date. Most people fail here — not because Claude can't do the work, but because they never wrote the offer down concretely enough for a buyer to say yes.

## The offer equation

Every strong offer answers four questions in one sentence:

**Result + Timeframe + Mechanism + Guarantee**

- **Result**: the outcome the buyer actually wants (not the deliverable — the outcome). Not "a blog post," but "content that keeps your email list opening your newsletter."
- **Timeframe**: when they get it. Concrete, not "soon." "Within 3 business days."
- **Mechanism**: what makes you credible to deliver it — your process, tool, or method. "Using a structured research-and-draft process with Claude" is honest and specific; "using AI" alone is not a mechanism, it's a buzzword.
- **Guarantee**: what happens if it's not good. Removes the buyer's risk. Doesn't have to be "money back" — can be "unlimited revisions until you approve" or "I only invoice on delivery."

Template: *"I'll deliver [result] within [timeframe] using [mechanism], and [guarantee]."*

## Five example offers, written in full

**1. Freelance services (Lane 1) — LinkedIn ghostwriting**
"I'll write and schedule 12 LinkedIn posts in your voice, delivered as a ready-to-post batch every two weeks, using a structured interview-plus-draft-plus-edit process so it sounds like you, not like a chatbot — and I'll revise any post that doesn't feel right until it does, at no extra charge."

**2. Freelance services (Lane 1) — research-to-brief service**
"I'll turn your raw meeting notes and scattered docs into a clean, decision-ready one-page brief within 24 hours, using a structured extraction-and-verification pass so nothing important gets lost or invented — and if the brief misses your key point, I'll redo it same-day."

**3. Digital product (Lane 2) — niche template pack**
"A 20-template Notion pack for freelance photographers to manage bookings, contracts, and invoices, ready to duplicate and use in under 10 minutes — lifetime updates included, and a full refund within 14 days if it doesn't fit your workflow."

**4. Local business services (Lane 4) — review-response retainer**
"I'll write and post a personalized response to every new Google/Yelp review your business gets, within 24 hours of it appearing, so no review sits unanswered — flat monthly rate, cancel anytime with 7 days' notice."

**5. Content-to-offer (Lane 3) — niche newsletter into a paid audit**
"A weekly newsletter breaking down one local marketing mistake and fix for independent gym owners, building toward a paid 45-minute marketing audit call for subscribers who want it applied to their business — first audit free for the first 5 replies, to build case studies."

Notice: none of these say "AI-powered" as the selling point. The buyer doesn't care what tool you use. They care about the result, the timeframe, and their risk. Mechanism and tooling stay in the fine print, not the headline.

## Pricing psychology, briefly

Three things matter more than the number itself:

- **Anchoring**: the first price a buyer sees sets their frame for every price after it. Show your highest tier first if you're presenting multiple options.
- **Framing as investment vs. cost**: price relative to what the buyer loses by not solving the problem (a missed lead, an unanswered review, hours of their own time), not relative to your hourly rate. Nobody buys "3 hours of your time." They buy "my inbox handled."
- **Round numbers vs. precise numbers**: round numbers ($500) read as a strategic package; precise numbers ($487) read as a cost buildup. Use round numbers for productized offers, precise/itemized for custom scope-of-work quotes.

For actual dollar figures: check current typical asking prices for comparable services on Upwork, Fiverr, or in relevant Facebook/Slack communities before you set yours — rates vary by niche, region, and platform, and any number printed in a playbook goes stale. **VERIFY: current rate ranges in your specific niche before finalizing your price.** As a starting posture: price at or slightly above the median of what you find for comparable scope, not the floor — racing to the bottom trains buyers to expect cheap and makes you compete on price alone.

## The three-tier structure: floor / anchor / premium

Never offer just one price. Three tiers do three jobs: the floor gets hesitant buyers in the door, the anchor is what you actually want most people to pick, and the premium makes the anchor look reasonable while capturing buyers who'd pay more anyway.

- **Floor**: smallest usable version of the offer. Exists to convert price-sensitive buyers and to be an easy "yes" for a first-time client. Design it so it's still profitable for your time, not a loss-leader.
- **Anchor**: the version you build your business around and push in conversation. Most complete, most reasonable value-for-price. This is the one you should be able to describe in one sentence without hedging.
- **Premium**: adds speed, exclusivity, or a done-with-you element (a call, priority turnaround, extra revisions). Priced high enough that a chunk of buyers choosing the anchor instead feels like a discount to them.

Concrete shape, applied to example offer #1 (LinkedIn ghostwriting):
- Floor: 8 posts/month, standard turnaround — lower price point.
- Anchor: 12 posts/month + monthly strategy call — your named "flagship" price.
- Premium: 16 posts/month + priority 48-hour turnaround + a comment-engagement pass — highest price point.

Set the floor at roughly 60–70% of the anchor's price and the premium at roughly 150–180% of the anchor's price — as a starting structure to adjust once you have real client feedback, not a fixed rule. **VERIFY: adjust these ratios based on what your actual buyers respond to once you have a handful of real conversations.**

Write your own three tiers now, in the same sentence format as the equation above, before moving to chapter 3. Delivery only makes sense once the offer is locked.
